01

Healing

Innocence · Courage · Unfearing

पूर्णम्

There is a place inside you that has never been hurt.

Purnam means full, or whole. It is the name Vivek gives his healing work, which he offers one-to-one under the name Avimukt. Three words sit at its centre: innocence, courage, unfearing.

You may have built a good life on top of an old pain. You train harder, read more, try the next method. The pain waits.

Through, not around.

Vivek’s healing work starts from one belief: you cannot think your way out. The body remembers, so the body is where you go. “I have walked it,” he says.

Try it now · one minute

The First Exercise

This is where the path inward begins. It needs nothing but your two hands and one quiet minute.

60
  1. Bring the five fingertips of one hand to the five fingertips of the other.
  2. Make the pressure equal at all five points.
  3. Do not push. Do not twist. Do not let one finger lead.
  4. Now feel all five points at the same time. Just feel.

This work supports your wellbeing. It is not medical treatment and does not replace care from a doctor or therapist.

02

Coaching

Work in · Live in the moment · Return to centre

The search for personal integrity.

Vivek calls his coaching practice Spiral. In his own words: “The practice of what I now call Spiral, the search for personal integrity, has really transformed my life.” He now walks the same path with others, one to one.

  • Work in. The practice showed him “what working in feels like, as opposed to working out”.
  • Live in the moment. “We have to start living in the moment and not in the momentum.”
  • Return to centre. “As goes your body, so goes your mind.”
Awareness, he says, is “like a tree: it can only go as high as its roots can go deep.”Vivek Chaturvedi, 2010

Hear him talk about it in three short videos from 2010, over his own music: Spiral One, Spiral Two and Spiral Three.

Ask about coaching

Coaching supports your awareness and wellbeing. It is not medical or psychological treatment.

03

Design

Find the root · Ask nature · Build it

time to reinvent the wheel

Shell, animal, bones · 1 of 3

We build things like a tortoise.

Look at a car. A helmet. A boot. Each one is a hard shell with a soft person inside.

A shell is good at one thing: sitting still and taking a hit. It is heavy. It is slow. And it does nothing to make the body inside it stronger.

Shell, animal, bones · 2 of 3

Then we ask them to run like a bison.

A bison weighs close to a tonne. It can run as fast as a horse, turn hard, and hit head-on without hurting itself.

It has no shell at all. So where does that strength come from?

Shell, animal, bones · 3 of 3

The strength is inside.

A skull plate that takes huge impacts. Shoulder blades that carry the ribs. Light legs that spring. The frame is on the inside and the soft parts are on the outside. You are built the same way.

“Why is a car like a tortoise? And yet we expect it to run like a bison.”Vivek Chaturvedi

The method · Aadivaahan

Nature solved it first.
He translates.

Aadivaahan is Vivek’s design house. It takes one everyday object at a time, finds the wrong idea buried in it, and redesigns it the way a living body would. आदि means “the origin”.

  1. Find the root. What does everyone assume about this object?
  2. Ask nature. How does a body, a tree or an animal already solve it?
  3. Build it. Make a working model with modern materials.
  4. Share it. License the design to companies who can make it at scale.

A chariot, a carriage, a steam train, a space shuttle. All of them still roll on the same old idea.

Footware · spelled that way on purpose

Every shoe protects the foot. None of them trains it.

Modern shoes are built around landing on the heel. The heel hits, the shock travels up the bones, and the arch, calf and Achilles (the body’s own springs) mostly sit it out.

Vivek’s view: the real pivot of the foot is not the heel. It is the small gap between the big toe and the second toe.

A picture of the idea, not a measurement.

Footware

India knew this a long time ago.

This is a paduka (पादुका), the oldest footwear in India. One small post, held between the first two toes. To keep it on, your toes must grip. That grip wakes up the whole chain: toes, arch, calf, knee, hip, spine.

Footware takes that old idea and rebuilds it with today’s materials, first for sport. A first filing followed in 2007.

Stage: being built, to be tested properly.

An honest note: research on how foot-strike affects injury is still mixed. That is the reason Footware is being built to be tested properly, with independent measurement. The pictures show a bare foot and a traditional paduka, not the product.

Heaven-met · a helmet turned inside out

Your head already wears the best helmet ever made.

Nature protects the brain in four layers, and it puts the soft ones on the outside.

A normal helmet does the reverse: hard outside, soft inside. Heaven-met turns the helmet around, so the soft part that soaks up the blow is on the outside.

Stage: designed. A first prototype is the next step.

The redwood · the vibration damper

A hundred metres tall. Thousands of years old. Still standing.

California’s redwoods have lived through centuries of earthquakes. They do not fight the shaking. Their shape lets it travel, spread out and fade.

Vivek’s Universal Vibration Damper borrows that shape. The same idea can be made small, for a cricket bat, a racquet or a bicycle, or large, for a building.

Press it and watch the tree.

Also on the bench: a quieter, more efficient propeller. A first model has been built and tested. Further out: a very light personal flying pack, starting with a quarter-scale model.

Sundancer · by aadimoto

A three-wheeler that makes its own electricity.

Big batteries make electric vehicles heavy. Sundancer goes the other way. A small, light engine does only one job: it runs a generator. The wheels are always driven by an electric motor.

  1. Small engineRuns at its best speed.
  2. GeneratorMakes electricity.
  3. Small batteryStores a little, gives a lot.
  4. Electric motorTurns the rear wheel.

2 seats. 3 wheels. Stage: designed. First prototype to come.

The same power unit is being designed to fit boats, light aircraft and cargo drones.

The years

How he got here

    Joins the Indian Navy

    The better part of twelve years, from cadet to lieutenant, as an electrical engineering officer. He learns how machines really work, and how they fail.

  2. 1994

    Aide-de-camp to the Governor of Karnataka

    Republic Day, 1994. Vivek stands in naval whites as aide-de-camp to the Governor, the last posting of his Navy years. A white uniform, a parade ground, a perfect form. Twenty years later he shows this photo on a TEDx stage, as one of the forms he once lived by.

  3. 1995

    An MBA in the United States

    Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, New York. Technology and new products.

  4. 1997

    United Technologies, then Silicon Valley

    Corporate technology strategy at one of America's largest engineering groups. Then the dot-com boom, from the inside. On a California beach he meets a stranger whose juggling inspires him.

  5. 2003

    He starts “un-performing”

    The dress codes go first. Then the career ladder. He begins to question every form he has been living by.

  6. 2006

    Everything falls away

    What looked like an ending became the start of the real work: the body, and language. Back in India, he leads the Indian arm of a Japanese mobile technology company through the early mobile boom.

  7. 2007

    A first filing

    The shoe idea that will become Footware goes on paper.

  8. 2008

    Aadividya: the manifesto

    He writes down the design philosophy that everything since has grown from: design for a creature with its bones on the inside.

  9. 2010

    Aadivaahan begins

    He starts Aadivaahan, a design house for human-centred products, and begins writing online.

  10. 2011

    Music, and a film

    By now he has been recording made-up-on-the-spot music for years. He makes a film, Aadividya.

  11. 2012

    “The story of how I got here”

    Fifty minutes, one whiteboard, one marker: a video he described as telling “the story of how I got here”. That January he also releases a book, PATH, with a film, Aadividya, and an album, Aadinaadam.

  12. 2014

    The Unperformance

    On a TEDx stage in Bengaluru he gives the idea a name: The Unperformance.

  13. 2016

    Three short films. In the first he tells how he became, in his words, “the perpetual rebel”.

  14. Today

    Building, and healing

    Prototypes for Footware, Heaven-met and Sundancer. And one-to-one healing work under the name Avimukt.

One mind, many hands

A polymath. Or a Renaissance man, in the old sense.

One mind that works across many crafts, with nature as its teacher. Vivek has worked in very different rooms. A ship’s engine room. A governor’s house. A strategy office in America. A design bench in Bengaluru. A music room. A healing room. What joins them is one habit of mind. He looks at how nature already does a thing, and then makes it again, in steel, in sound, in colour or in words.

06

Music

Guitar · Tanpura tuning · No script

He plays what is in his bones.

Vivek has played music all his life. He never took lessons on any instrument. “All the instruments and voices are me.”

A string can only move in whole loops. One, two, three. Every note he plays is made of these.

One pluck. Then it settles.

His music, in his words

Tuned like a tanpura.

For years he tried to play rock. “I spent about 30 years trying to be a rock star,” he told a TEDx audience in 2014.

In America his mother brought him an electronic tanpura, the drone that sits under Indian classical music. He left it playing for years. “This tanpura is the canvas on which music is written.”

One day he tuned his guitar to it, and the music came loose. He plays themes, not songs. “I’ve never tried to play songs as much as I’m trying to explore themes, which is what rags are.” Nothing is scripted. Every piece is found as he plays it.

He plays guitar, bansuri and other flutes, a Native American flute, cajon, djembe, tabla, didgeridoo, a Vietnamese jaw harp, keyboard, and voice. On the TEDx stage he played the jaw harp, made from the brass of old bombs: “from a bomb to music”.

“My guitar first of all is tuned like a tanpura or a sitar.”
“Muscle memory is never a gift, always a practice.”
“Aadinaadam is original sound.”

Where everything on this page comes from

Vivek takes what nature and old makers already worked out, and translates it. This page is made the same way.

  • Dots. Every figure here is made of dots of light, like a kolam, where the dots come first too.
  • The balls. The six balls he juggles, and the discs that open each chapter, are cut from his own paintings.
  • Colour. The chapter colours are old Indian ones: haldi (turmeric), neel (indigo), geru (red earth), madder root and lamp black.
  • The lamp. Press the lamp at the top and the room changes between day and night.
  • The thread. One line runs down the page from the first chapter to the last. He calls himself a sutra-dhaar, a holder of the thread.
  • Type. Instrument Serif and Instrument Sans. Sanskrit words are set in Tiro Devanagari Sanskrit.
